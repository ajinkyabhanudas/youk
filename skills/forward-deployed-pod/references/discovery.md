# Discovery

Owned by the **Field Lead**. In a project with no users, this is the highest-leverage phase
available — and it's the one nearly everyone skips, which is exactly why doing it well
differentiates.

The forward-deployed competency being demonstrated: **extracting a workflow from a stranger's
head**. Not validating your idea. If you leave an interview with your prior intact, you probably
asked leading questions.

---

## Choosing the interview type

| Type | Use when | Core question |
|------|----------|---------------|
| **Discovery** | You don't yet know the workflow | "Walk me through the last time you…" |
| **Switch** | Something already exists and you want to know why people move | "What made you stop using X?" |
| **Contextual inquiry** | The work is hard to describe but easy to watch | "Do it while I watch, narrate as you go" |
| **Evaluative** | You have something to put in front of them | "Try this. Think aloud." |

Switch interviews are underused and produce the sharpest material, because the moment of change
is where people remember actual causes rather than reconstructed preferences.

---

## Recruiting

- **8–12 conversations** is where themes usually stabilise for a narrow segment. Below 5, you
  have anecdotes.
- **Screen for recency and behaviour, not opinion.** "Have you done X in the last 30 days?" beats
  "Are you interested in X?"
- **Segment deliberately** and note it — 9 interviews across 3 unrelated segments is 3 samples of
  3, not a sample of 9.
- Sources: existing communities in the problem space, second-degree contacts, relevant subreddits
  and Discords, cold outreach with a specific ask and a stated time bound.

---

## Question design

**Questions that work** — past-tense, specific instance, behaviour:

- "Walk me through the last time this came up. Start from what you were doing beforehand."
- "What did you do next?" (repeat until the workflow is complete)
- "What did you try before that? Why did you stop?"
- "What happened after you got the answer? Where did it go?"
- "How long did that take? What did it cost you?"
- "Who else was involved?"
- "When did you first notice this was a problem?"
- "What would have had to be true for you to not have that problem?"

**Questions that poison the data** — hypothetical, future-tense, feature-shaped:

- "Would you use a tool that…" (everyone says yes)
- "How much would you pay for…" (stated willingness to pay is near-worthless)
- "Do you think this is a good idea?"
- "Wouldn't it be better if…"
- Any question containing your solution

**The rule:** if the question contains the answer you're hoping for, rewrite it.

---

## In the room

- **Record and transcribe**, with consent. You cannot code what you didn't capture, and note-
  taking makes you a worse listener.
- **Talk under 20% of the time.** Count it afterwards; most people are shocked.
- **Chase specifics.** "Usually" and "generally" are signals to redirect: "Tell me about the most
  recent time specifically."
- **Sit in silence.** The three seconds after someone finishes is where the real answer arrives.
- **Follow surprise, not your guide.** The moments where you think "wait, why would they do
  that?" are the findings.
- **Don't pitch.** If you must show something, do it in the last five minutes, after the workflow
  questions are done.

**Sensitive domains** (fertility, health, finance): explicit consent for recording, a clear
statement of what happens to the data, permission to skip anything, and no clinical framing you
aren't qualified to give. Note the consent process in your write-up — it's part of the evidence
of professionalism.

---

## Synthesis without inventing themes

1. **Extract observations, not conclusions.** One per line, in the interviewee's own framing,
   tagged with source. "P4: exports to CSV, re-sorts manually every Monday."
2. **Cluster bottom-up.** Group observations that co-occur. Name the cluster only after it exists.
   Naming first produces the themes you expected.
3. **Count unprompted mentions.** A theme raised spontaneously by 6 of 9 is real; a theme that
   only appeared when you asked about it directly is your theme, not theirs.
4. **Record disconfirming evidence explicitly.** Every synthesis should have a section for what
   contradicted the hypothesis. Its absence means it wasn't looked for.
5. **Keep the outliers visible.** One person doing something completely different is often the
   most informative data point in the set.

**Exit test for this phase:** you found at least one thing that surprised you. If not, the
interviews were confirmation-seeking and the synthesis is unreliable.

---

## Output format

```
## Discovery synthesis — [segment], n=9, [dates]

**Method:** [type], [duration], recorded with consent, coded bottom-up.
**Segment:** [precise description + how recruited]

### What surprised us
[Lead with this. It's the most valuable section and the one that proves the work was real.]

### Themes
**[Theme]** — 6/9 unprompted
> [short verbatim, ≤15 words]
Implication: […]

### Disconfirming evidence
[What contradicted the hypothesis, and what we're doing about it]

### Workflow map
[The actual sequence of steps people take today, including the workarounds]

### What we're not concluding
[Where n is too thin, where the segment was too narrow]
```

That last section is the one that reads as senior. Everyone lists findings; almost nobody bounds
them.
